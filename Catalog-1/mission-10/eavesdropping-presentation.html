Mission-10 · Eavesdropping

Eavesdropping and the Padlock

How attackers read unencrypted connections, how they read encrypted ones, and why a closed padlock is necessary but not sufficient.

Plain HTTP

Unencrypted = postcard on the bus

Passive sniffingAnyone on the same Wi-Fi/LAN captures packets with Wireshark. No noise, hard to notice.
Man-in-the-middleARP spoofing, fake "free airport Wi-Fi", evil twin. Attacker sits between you and server, reads everything.
Session hijackingCaptured cookies or tokens let the attacker act as the victim. No password needed.
Observers on the pathISPs, router admins, proxies: all plain traffic is readable.
Malware on the deviceA trojan reads data before it ever leaves the machine.

What leaks: passwords, cookies, tokens, messages, form input, full URLs and metadata.

TLS / HTTPS

Encrypted: ciphertext unreadable, everything else fair game

Traffic analysisAttacker sees who talks to whom, when, how much. Patterns and IPs still identify sites and actions.
Key theftNobody breaks AES. Attackers steal keys from memory: Heartbleed (2014) leaked keys and cookies.
Endpoint malwareMalware reads data before encryption and after decryption. Crypto bypassed, not broken.
Downgrade attacksTrick parties into weak SSL (POODLE, SSLv2). Modern TLS 1.3 refuses to fall back.
Fake-certificate MITMAttacker decrypts/re-encrypts with own cert. Works only if the victim accepts it: planted root CA, ignored warning.
PhishingNo crypto attack at all: trick the human, and the secret walks out. Encryption carries stolen data safely.

Key insight: attacking the math is hopeless. Real attacks hit the weak points around the crypto: metadata, keys in memory, endpoints, downgrades, humans.

Metadata

What an eavesdropper still sees under TLS

# observer on the path (Wi-Fi router, ISP) without breaking encryption: whom you talk to destination IP + port when and how often timestamps, sizes, timing what you open approx. size/type of content (site fingerprint) what they don't see: message content, passwords, cookies
Who talks to whom and how much stays visible even in the strongest TLS 1.3 connection. Encryption hides content, not existence.

Evaluation

Take the claim apart

🔒

"A closed padlock means the connection is secure and the visited domain is authentic."

What is trueTraffic to the domain is encrypted, and the certificate matches the domain in the address bar. That part is real.
The claim goes too far"Secure" and "authentic" in the everyday sense mean much more than the padlock certifies. It is necessary, but not sufficient.

Padlock reality check

Flip between what it tells and what it hides

Why "authentic" fails

Every scammer can have a padlock

🔒 https://paypal-secure-login.example.com/?account=verify # Let's Encrypt issues certificates free and automatic: # to everyone, including fraudsters. Valid cert, real padlock. # Certificate proves: this domain matches its own certificate. # Nothing more.
Lookalike domainspaypa1.com, paypal-support.net: padlock + wrong domain.
Trust-seal misreadingSurveys: many users read the lock as "honest business". It never promised that.
Ignored warningsA clicked-away certificate error defeats the whole point.

Conclusion

Padlock = encryption to that domain name, nothing more

ClaimVerdict
Traffic encryptedTrue
Domain matches certificateTrue, technical sense
Site content safeNo, malware and fraud ride encrypted
Operator honestNo, fraudsters get valid certs
Connection cannot be eavesdroppedNo, metadata, endpoints, keys, humans remain
Padlock necessary, not sufficient

Trust comes from checking the domain itself: exact name, no lookalike, no ignored warning. The lock only says the connection to whatever domain you actually visited is encrypted.
